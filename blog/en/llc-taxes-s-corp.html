LLC and taxes: when does an S-Corp election make sense?

Forming an LLC does not by itself mean paying less tax. How it is taxed by default and when it is worth considering the S-Corp election.

ISM Consulting TeamNovember 2026 · 6 min read

Key takeaways

  • By default, a single-owner LLC is taxed on Schedule C and a multi-owner LLC as a partnership.
  • The S-Corp election can reduce self-employment tax, but adds payroll and paperwork.
  • An owner who works in their S-Corp must pay themselves a reasonable salary.
  • There is no magic profit amount: it depends on each business.

An LLC protects your personal assets from business debts, but the IRS has no special tax category for it. How it is taxed depends on how many owners it has and on what you choose.

How an LLC is taxed by default

  • Single-owner LLC: treated as part of your personal return (Schedule C). Profits pay income tax and self-employment tax.
  • LLC with two or more owners: treated as a partnership. It files Form 1065 and each owner receives a Schedule K-1.

What is the S-Corp election?

It is a way of being taxed that an LLC can choose by filing Form 2553. It is not a different type of company, but a tax decision. With it, the owner who works in the business receives a salary with withholdings, and the rest of the profits are paid out as distributions, which do not pay self-employment tax.

Possible advantages

  • It can reduce self-employment tax on the portion of profit that is not salary.

What changes or costs more

  • You must pay yourself a reasonable salary, with payroll, withholdings and quarterly and annual forms.
  • An additional business return (Form 1120-S) is filed every year.
  • There is more bookkeeping and higher preparation costs.
  • Some states charge additional taxes or fees to S-Corps.

When can it make sense?

Normally when the business generates steady profits large enough for the savings to outweigh the costs and extra work. There is no single amount that works for everyone: it depends on your profit, your state and what salary would be reasonable.

Before deciding

  • Calculate your net profit for recent months.
  • Compare the tax with and without the election.
  • Ask about payroll costs and your state’s taxes.
  • Keep in mind there are deadlines to file the election.
This is a general guide. The right decision is made with your business’s numbers. Ask us for a review.
