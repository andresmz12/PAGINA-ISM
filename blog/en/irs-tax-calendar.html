Tax calendar: the IRS dates you cannot afford to miss

Personal returns, estimated taxes, W-2 and 1099 forms, extensions and businesses: the key dates of the year to avoid penalties and interest.

ISM Consulting TeamNovember 2026 · 5 min read

Key takeaways

  • The usual deadline to file as an individual is April 15.
  • An extension gives you more time to file, not more time to pay.
  • If you work for yourself, you probably need to pay estimated taxes each quarter.
  • If a date falls on a weekend or holiday, it moves to the next business day.

Missing an IRS deadline almost always costs money: penalties for filing late, penalties for paying late, and interest. The good news is that the main dates are few and repeat every year. This guide summarizes them for a tax year ending December 31, which is the case for most people and small businesses.

January: income forms

  • About January 15: the fourth estimated tax payment of the prior year is due, for those who pay them.
  • January 31: employers must give Form W-2 to employees, and businesses must give Form 1099-NEC to the contractors they paid.

March: partnerships and S-Corp elections

March 15 is generally the deadline for partnership returns (Form 1065) and S corporation returns (Form 1120-S). This includes LLCs with more than one owner that are taxed as partnerships, and LLCs that elected to be taxed as an S-Corp.

April: the most important date

  • April 15: individual returns (Form 1040) are due, including single-member LLC owners and self-employed workers. C corporations and the first estimated tax payment of the year are also due.

June, September and October

  • June 15: second estimated tax payment.
  • September 15: third estimated tax payment.
  • October 15: deadline if you requested an extension to file your individual return.

The extension: more time to file, not to pay

With Form 4868 you can get an automatic extension to file your individual return. But payment of any tax you owe is still due on the original date. If you do not pay on time, interest and penalties apply even with an extension. That is why you should estimate what you owe before April.

Your state counts too

Besides the IRS, most states have their own return, with dates that normally match the federal ones but can vary. If you own a business, there may also be annual reports and state fees.

Dates can change because of holidays, natural disasters or other IRS rules. Always confirm the current date for your case.
